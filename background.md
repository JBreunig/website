## Engineering the developing and diseased brain

The Breunig Lab studies how neural stem and progenitor cells acquire specific identities during development, how those programs are altered in brain cancer, and how they can be deliberately manipulated for biological discovery and therapeutic development.

Our work sits at the intersection of **developmental neurobiology, cancer biology, synthetic biology, genome engineering, and single-cell and spatial genomics**. Rather than relying on a single model system, we develop complementary experimental platforms that allow us to move from mechanism to perturbation to therapeutic testing.

## Somatic and genome engineering

We develop genetic technologies that enable precise manipulation of cells in vivo and in three-dimensional models. These approaches allow us to introduce defined combinations of oncogenic or developmental perturbations, trace cell lineages, alter cell states, and test gene function in experimentally controlled settings.

## Models of brain development and cancer

We use genetically defined mouse models, somatic engineering approaches, primary and stem-cell-derived systems, and human iPSC-derived cerebral organoids to study neural development and pediatric and adult brain tumors. These systems are designed to capture both the initiating genetic events and the evolving cellular states that accompany tumor progression.

## Single-cell and spatial biology

Single-cell RNA sequencing, multiomic profiling, and spatial technologies let us resolve developmental trajectories, tumor heterogeneity, and interactions among malignant, neural, immune, and stromal populations. We integrate these datasets with functional perturbation experiments so that descriptive cell states can be connected to causal mechanisms.

## From mechanism to therapy

A central goal of the laboratory is to use mechanistic insight to identify vulnerabilities that can be exploited therapeutically. We are particularly interested in developmental programs, cell-state transitions, metabolism, tumor–microenvironment interactions, and programmable genetic strategies that may enable more selective treatment of brain tumors.
